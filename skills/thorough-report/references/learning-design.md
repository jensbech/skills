# Learning design: a report someone can learn from

A report can be accurate, well written and free of repetition and still teach badly, because the reader was never asked to do anything with what they read and met ideas in an order that suited the subject, not the learner. This file is the orchestrator's guide to planning for learning in Phase 2. The writer-facing rules that follow from it are in the writing guide template.

## Outcomes come first

In Phase 0 you named three to six things the reader will be able to do after the report. Good outcomes are things a person does: explain why a price moves, read a bill or a filing, estimate an order of magnitude, tell two roles apart, ask an informed question. "Understand the market" is a topic, not an outcome.

Use them three ways:

- **Chapter plan.** Give each chapter one to three outcomes of its own that add up to the report's. A chapter that serves none is background; shorten it or cut it.
- **Preface.** Print the report's outcomes, so the reader knows what they are working towards.
- **Final check.** The test reader says whether they can now do each one.

## The ladder

Order concepts so that each rests only on earlier ones.

- List the concepts the outcomes require, then what each of those requires, until you reach what the reader already knows. That list, ordered, is the skeleton of the chapter plan and the term rows of the registry.
- A row's home chapter must not come after the first chapter that depends on it. When the subject forces a forward reference, the earlier chapter gets a short apposition and a pointer, and the registry says so.
- Start from the reader's own experience of the subject (the bill they pay, the product they use, the news story they remember) and work inward to the machinery. History and institutional detail come after the reader knows what they are the history of.

## One running case

Pick one concrete case and follow it through the whole report: a household, a shipment, a patient, one mid-sized company, a single transaction. Chapter 1 introduces it; each later chapter shows what that chapter's mechanism does to it.

- It gives the reader a fixed point, so each new idea attaches to something already familiar.
- It replaces invented examples. Build it from sourced figures in the notes (typical consumption, a published tariff, a median size). Where it needs arithmetic of your own, the text shows the arithmetic and calls the case illustrative.
- It is a registry row with chapter 1 as home. Later chapters advance the case; they don't reintroduce it.
- Give each writer its part: "in your chapter, the case meets X; show Y".

## Order inside a section

Writers explain in this order, and the guide tells them so:

1. **The case or the observation.** Something the reader can picture.
2. **The mechanism.** What is happening and who does what.
3. **The name.** The field's term arrives after the reader has seen the thing it names.
4. **The number.** Dated, with its scale made tangible by a comparison.
5. **The consequence.** What follows, what goes wrong, or what it changes for the running case.

Two more devices carry a lot of teaching for little space:

- **Worked examples.** Anything with a formula, a bill, a threshold or a procedure gets one example worked through with real numbers. Each worked example is a story row in the registry, so it is worked once.
- **Misconceptions.** Researchers record what newcomers usually get wrong. Give each chapter the one or two that belong to it; they go in the note box, stated as the mistaken belief followed by what is true.

## Figures that build

The master figure in chapter 1 shows the whole system. Later figures zoom into parts of it and keep its positions, symbols and flow encodings, so that the reader recognises where they are. The chapter-opener map does the same job at small scale. A figure that redraws a part of the system in a new layout forces the reader to learn it twice.

## Questions instead of recaps

A chapter-end summary re-teaches, which the report forbids. A question makes the reader retrieve and use what they just read, which is what makes it stay. From Standard tier up, every chapter ends with a check-yourself box; a Briefing has one box of five questions at the end.

A good set for a chapter:

- one **why** question (explain a mechanism in your own words);
- one **what-if** question (predict what happens when something changes);
- one **case** question (apply the chapter to the running case, with numbers the chapter supplied);
- from chapter 3 on, one question that needs something from an **earlier chapter** as well.

Questions that ask for a definition or a number to be recalled are not worth the space. Each answer is one or two sentences, uses only what the report has taught so far, and sits at the end of the chapter after the sources. The writer of the chapter writes both.

## Reading routes

The preface offers two or three routes through the report: the short one (summary, chapter 1, the reader-specific chapter), the full one in order, and, where it fits, a route for a reader who already knows the fundamentals. It also says in one sentence how to use the questions.

## The test reader

Reviewers who know the notes can't tell where a newcomer gets lost. The test reader is a fresh agent that plays the target reader, sees only the PDF text, and reads in order. It reports terms used before they were taught, things taught twice, questions the text doesn't let it answer, answers that disagree with the text, and whether it can now do each outcome. Its "lost here" findings are usually ladder faults: fix them by moving an explanation earlier or adding an apposition, not by explaining twice.

## By tier

| | Briefing | Standard | Comprehensive and Exhaustive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcomes | 3 | 3–5 | 4–6 |
| Running case | yes, light | yes | yes, with a worked example in most chapters |
| Questions | 5 at the end | 3 per chapter | 3–5 per chapter |
| Reading routes | none needed | 2 | 2–3 |
| Test reader | folded into the final reviewer | separate agent | separate agent |
