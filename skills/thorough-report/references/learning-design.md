# Learning design: a report someone can learn from

A report can be accurate, well written and free of repetition and still teach badly, because the reader was never asked to do anything with what they read and met ideas in an order that suited the subject, not the learner. This file is the orchestrator's guide to planning for learning in Phase 2. The writer-facing rules that follow from it are in the writing guide template.

## The reader's job: study or guide

Decide in Phase 0 what the reader will do with the report, because it changes the structure, the density and the furniture. Ask: when they finish, will they mostly explain the subject, or do something in their own life or work?

- **Study.** The reader is learning a field: an industry, a technology, a market, a body of law. They will explain it, read its documents and judge claims about it. They read in order.
- **Guide.** The reader has a situation and wants to know what to do: a parent of a two-year-old, someone buying a first home, a new manager, a patient with a diagnosis. They arrive with a question, look up their own case and act on it. Subjects about people, health, family, habits and everyday decisions are nearly always guides, even when the user says "paper" or "report".

| | Study | Guide |
|---|---|---|
| Organised by | the subject's own structure, fundamentals to frontier | the situation the reader arrives with (their child's age, their stage, their budget, the decision in front of them) |
| First pages | a summary and an overview chapter with the master figure | a **start-here page** that answers the main question for each situation in a few lines and points to the chapter |
| A section goes | case, mechanism, name, number, consequence | situation, what to do, why it works, how firm the advice is, what to do when it doesn't go to plan |
| Numbers | dated and exact, with their backstory in one home | only the ones the reader acts on, rounded the way a professional says them aloud |
| Evidence | named in the text where it teaches | behind the citation; the text says in words which kind of claim this is |
| Figures | a master figure and figures that zoom into it, within the ceiling | few and plain: a timeline, a ladder, a decision path. No statistical charts |
| Boxes | key figures, note, lens, check yourself | note (a misconception, or when to get help), lens (something to try), decide |
| End of a chapter | check-yourself questions with answers | a decide box: two to four questions about the reader's own situation, each with what the answer means |
| Voice | a colleague explaining the field | the same colleague advising a friend: "you", plain verbs, no lecture |

A report is one or the other. A guide still rests on the same research, source checks and citations; it shows less of the apparatus.

### What a guide owes its reader

- **Their entry point is the structure.** Write down the situation the reader arrives with and make it the chapter axis. A parent arrives with an age, so the chapters are age bands and each band covers every area; a report with one chapter per area makes that parent assemble their own month from five chapters. The subject's own categories appear inside each chapter, in the same order every time, so one area can be followed across chapters.
- **The start-here page.** One or two pages straight after the preface, as the first chapter file, unnumbered. Its words count towards the tier's total. For each situation: what to start now, what to keep doing, what can wait, in a few short lines, with the chapter number. A reader who stops there has still been helped. It is a list per situation, not a grid. It may carry one orientation figure, which counts against the ceiling. A reader who wants the reasons follows the pointer; the entry test allows them that one chapter.
- **Three kinds of claim, told apart in words.** What an authority recommends ("the health authorities say"), what research found and how firmly ("most children", "studies point both ways"), and what is custom or the reader's own choice ("no authority sets an age; this is yours to decide"). The reader should never have to guess which kind they are reading. Where the report gives its own reading of thin evidence, it says so.
- **Evidence stays backstage.** Study names, sample sizes, cohorts, percentages and comparisons between studies live in the notes and behind the citation. The text carries what follows from them. A number appears when the reader would act on it or misjudge without it: an age range, a dose, a price, a deadline.
- **Every section ends in something the reader can do**, and says when to stop trying and ask a professional.
- **Things the user asked about that fall outside the reader's range** (a skill that comes years later, an option that doesn't apply in their country) get a short, plain answer in the closing chapter, so the reader knows they can stop wondering.
- **The closing chapter**, if there is one, holds what cuts across the situations: the few principles behind the advice. It is short, applies and doesn't recap (the reader-specific contract in `repetition.md`), and ends with a decide box like the others.

## Outcomes come first

In Phase 0 you named the things the reader will be able to do after the report (three at Briefing, up to six above). Good outcomes are things a person does: explain why a price moves, read a bill or a filing, estimate an order of magnitude, tell two roles apart, ask an informed question; in a guide, choose what to start now, tell firm advice from custom, know when to get help. "Understand the market" is a topic, not an outcome.

Use them three ways:

- **Chapter plan.** Give each chapter one to three outcomes of its own that add up to the report's. A chapter that serves none is background; shorten it or cut it.
- **Preface.** Print the report's outcomes, so the reader knows what they are working towards.
- **Final check.** The test reader says whether they can now do each one.

## The ladder

Order concepts so that each rests only on earlier ones.

- List the concepts the outcomes require, then what each of those requires, until you reach what the reader already knows. That list, ordered, is the skeleton of the chapter plan and the term rows of the registry.
- A row's home chapter must not come after the first chapter that depends on it. When the subject forces a forward reference, the earlier chapter gets a short apposition and a pointer, and the registry says so.
- Start from the reader's own experience of the subject (the bill they pay, the product they use, the news story they remember) and work inward to the machinery. History and institutional detail come after the reader knows what they are the history of.

## One running case

Pick one concrete case and follow it through the whole report: a household, a shipment, a patient, one mid-sized company, a single transaction. Chapter 1 introduces it; each later chapter shows what that chapter's mechanism does to it.

- It gives the reader a fixed point, so each new idea attaches to something already familiar.
- It replaces invented examples. Build it from sourced figures in the notes (typical consumption, a published tariff, a median size). Where it needs arithmetic of your own, the text shows the arithmetic and calls the case illustrative.
- It is a registry row with chapter 1 as home. Later chapters advance the case; they don't reintroduce it.
- Give each writer its part: "in your chapter, the case meets X; show Y".
- In a guide, the case is one person in the reader's position who moves through the situations (one child growing through the age bands), and each chapter may open with them in two or three sentences. It stays light: the reader came for their own case, not the example's.

## Order inside a section

In a study report, writers explain in this order, and the writing guide tells them so (a guide uses the order in the table above):

1. **The case or the observation.** Something the reader can picture.
2. **The mechanism.** What is happening and who does what.
3. **The name.** The field's term arrives after the reader has seen the thing it names.
4. **The number.** Dated, with its scale made tangible by a comparison.
5. **The consequence.** What follows, what goes wrong, or what it changes for the running case.

Two more devices carry a lot of teaching for little space:

- **Worked examples.** Anything with a formula, a bill, a threshold or a procedure gets one example worked through with real numbers. Each worked example is a story row in the registry, so it is worked once.
- **Misconceptions.** Researchers record what newcomers usually get wrong. Give each chapter the one or two that belong to it; they go in the note box, stated as the mistaken belief followed by what is true.

## Figures and tables earn their place

Prose is the default. A figure or a table is there because it shows the reader something faster than a paragraph could: how parts connect, an order with branches, a spread over time, a difference in size. The figure counts in SKILL.md are ceilings, and a chapter with no figure is normal. When planning, write next to each figure the one sentence the reader should take from it; a figure without that sentence is cut.

A figure passes when:

- **it has one message**, and the caption's first sentence states it;
- **a newcomer can read it in about ten seconds**, without the body text explaining how;
- it uses **at most three encodings** and its legend has at most four entries;
- it carries **at most about a dozen labels**;
- a chart shows **one source on one scale**. Different studies, populations or definitions never share an axis; if the comparison matters, the text makes it.

A figure that fails is split, simplified or turned back into a sentence. Reporting how thorough the research was is not a job for a figure.

In a study report, the master figure in chapter 1 shows the whole system. Later figures zoom into parts of it and keep its positions, symbols and encodings, so that the reader recognises where they are. A figure that redraws a part of the system in a new layout forces the reader to learn it twice. A guide needs no master figure; if it has an orientation figure, it is a plain timeline or path on the start-here page.

A table is for looking things up:

- a cell holds a word, a number or a phrase of about six words at most. Sentences in cells mean the content is prose; write it as short sections or a list;
- at most five columns;
- a summary table doesn't restate a figure, and a figure doesn't restate a table.

Key-figures boxes belong to study chapters that turn on numbers. A guide has none.

## Questions instead of recaps

A chapter-end summary re-teaches, which the report forbids. A question makes the reader retrieve and use what they just read, which is what makes it stay. In a study report from Standard tier up, every chapter ends with a check-yourself box; a Briefing has one box of five questions at the end.

A guide doesn't quiz its reader. Its chapters end with a **decide box**: two to four questions the reader answers about their own situation, each followed by what the answer means ("Does she stay dry for two hours? If yes, start; if not, look again in a month"). It has no answers block.

A good set for a chapter (pick as many kinds as the tier gives questions):

- one **why** question (explain a mechanism in your own words);
- one **what-if** question (predict what happens when something changes);
- one **case** question (apply the chapter to the running case, with numbers the chapter supplied);
- from chapter 3 on, one question that needs something from an **earlier chapter** as well.

Questions that ask for a definition or a number to be recalled are not worth the space. Each answer is one or two sentences, uses only what the report has taught so far, and sits at the end of the chapter after the sources. The writer of the chapter writes both.

## Reading routes

The preface of a study report offers two or three routes through the report: the short one (summary, chapter 1, the reader-specific chapter), the full one in order, and, where it fits, a route for a reader who already knows the fundamentals. It also says in one sentence how to use the questions. A guide needs one sentence instead: find your situation on the start-here page and go to its chapter.

## The test reader

Reviewers who know the notes can't tell where a newcomer gets lost. The test reader is a fresh agent that plays the target reader and sees only the PDF text.

It starts with the **entry test**, which Phase 2 prepares: three concrete readers written into the writing guide, each with a situation and a question ("her son is 26 months and shows no interest in the potty; should she start?"). For each, the test reader opens the report cold and records which pages it had to visit before it could answer. A guide passes when each reader has a usable answer from the start-here page and at most one chapter. A study report passes when the summary and chapter 1 let each reader say how the subject fits together and where their question is treated.

Then it reads in order and reports terms used before they were taught, things taught twice, questions the text doesn't let it answer, answers that disagree with the text, and whether it can now do each outcome. Its "lost here" findings are usually ladder faults: fix them by moving an explanation earlier or adding an apposition, not by explaining twice.
