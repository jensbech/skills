# Repetition: teach each thing once

Parallel writers each produce a chapter that is complete on its own, so a reader going cover to cover is taught the same things several times. It reads as padding and makes the reader doubt that anyone read the whole. On an unconsolidated 137-page report, `repetition.py` found 20 terms defined in two to four chapters and 22 stories retold, one of them in five chapters.

This file is for the orchestrator (Phase 2 and the gates) and the consolidation editor. The rules writers follow are in the writing guide template under "Teach once".

## Why it happens

1. **Writers can't see each other.** Each tells the full story of everything its chapter touches.
2. **Well-meant rules invite it.** "Every chapter should stand alone" and "explain each abbreviation on first use in every chapter" both produce it. The guide replaces them with the entry-point rule: a chapter's lead names what it builds on, with chapter numbers, and doesn't re-teach it.
3. **Bracket chapters.** An overview that previews every topic in full, a history chapter that re-explains today's mechanisms, a future chapter that restates current numbers with their backstory, a closing chapter that recaps.
4. **Seam topics without an owner.** A court ruling belongs to both the topical chapter and history; a subsidy to both the customer chapter and history. Unassigned, both tell it.
5. **Numbers travel with their backstory.** Repeating "74 companies" is fine. Repeating what was counted, by whom, and why another source says 79 is not.
6. **Review split by chapter group.** An editor who owns four chapters can't see what the other eight say.

Stories and number backstories cost the reader more than repeated terms do, and a term list alone doesn't catch them.

## Before writing: the registry and the contracts

**The registry** has 40–150 rows depending on tier, built from the research notes and the chapter plan. Each row has exactly one home chapter and one shared line of wording.

| Kind | Examples | What "home" means |
|---|---|---|
| Term | a role, a tariff component, an abbreviation | the only chapter with a margin note or a definition sentence |
| Story | a reform, a court case, a crisis, a launch and its uptake, a worked example, the running case | the only chapter that tells it with dates and details |
| Number backstory | what a key figure counts, its source, why sources disagree | the only chapter that explains the number; others just use it |

Assign every seam topic explicitly. When in doubt, a story goes to the chapter where it changes the reader's understanding of a mechanism, and history gets one sentence and a pointer.

Each writer's brief then lists two sets: its **home rows**, which it explains in full, and its **neighbour rows**, which are homed elsewhere but which the chapter will touch.

**Bracket-chapter contracts.** Paste the ones that apply into the guide, with chapter numbers, and into the brief of the writer who owns each chapter.

- **Overview or summary chapter.** It is a map. Each topic gets at most two sentences and a pointer to its home. No formulas, no source discrepancies, no worked examples. Margin notes only for terms homed here. The master figure carries the detail.
- **History chapter.** It owns events as a sequence. It names today's mechanisms without re-explaining them ("the income cap, chapter 6"). Topical chapters mention a historical event in one sentence with a pointer here, unless the registry gives them the story.
- **Future chapter.** It starts from current numbers by reference ("from the 2025 production figure in chapter 3") and spends its words on what changes, why, and how sure anyone is.
- **Reader-specific chapter.** It applies; it doesn't recap. Every paragraph states a consequence for the reader that no earlier chapter stated. Earlier facts appear as short references.

## After writing: consolidation

`repetition.py` writes a work list in five sections: (1) terms defined in several chapters, (2) near-duplicate sentences, (3) repeated facts, (4) reworded definitions, (5) retold passages, found by the numbers, dates and names that paragraphs share.

One consolidation editor owns every chapter file and works through the list in the order 5, 1, 4, 2, then rereads the bracket chapters against everything before them. The list is a work list, not a verdict: section 5 also links paragraphs that share figures for unrelated reasons, so each cluster is read before anything is cut.

## Gates

Check after consolidation and again before delivery, since editors and revisers can reintroduce repetition.

| Gate | Pass |
|---|---|
| §1 terms defined in several chapters | empty, or only cases the guide lists as deliberate |
| §2 near-duplicate sentences | no pair at 0.70 or above |
| §5 retold passages | no cluster spans three or more chapters; in each two-chapter cluster, one side is the full telling and the other is at most two sentences (check by eye) |
| Test reader and final reviewer | read cover to cover and find nothing taught twice |

## Don't overcorrect

- Keep a number where the local argument uses it. Cut the backstory around it.
- The target for a neighbour row is one sentence carrying the local point, plus a pointer. A paragraph that only says "see chapters 4, 6 and 9" fails the reader too.
- When a cut leaves a sentence unclear, add an apposition of eight words or fewer.
- Before deleting a non-home telling, move any detail only it had into the home chapter.
- Check-yourself questions may refer back to earlier chapters. That is retrieval, not re-teaching, and the script ignores those boxes.
